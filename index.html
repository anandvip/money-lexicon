Why Invent Words At All

A name turns a vague feeling into a thing you can notice

Before you have a word for a pattern, you can only describe it in a long sentence: "that thing where I save ₹50 on a coupon and then spend ₹2,000 because I feel I've earned it." Once the pattern has a name (thrift-splurge), you can spot it the moment it starts, mention it to someone in one breath, and keep track of how often it happens.

A good coinage works like a handle: it gives your attention something to grip.

This is why psychology keeps naming things, such as "anchoring" or "loss aversion". The words below do the same job for everyday habits, but they are made for your own use rather than for academic papers.

The Toolkit

Four ways the words in this lexicon were built

Every entry below uses one of these four techniques. Learn to recognise them and you can take apart any new word you meet, or put together one of your own.

Method 1 · Blend

Portmanteau

Take the front of one word and the back of another, ideally joining at a shared sound so the seam disappears.

spend + pendulum → spendulum
Method 2 · Hyphen

Compound

Keep both words whole and join them with a hyphen. It is less clever than a blend, but instantly readable.

cart + amnesia → cart-amnesia
Method 3 · Eponym

Borrowed name

Use a figure from myth or history as shorthand for a quality. The name brings its own story along.

Plutus (god of wealth) + flip
Method 4 · Affix family

Derivation

Once a root word exists, add standard English endings to grow a family of nouns and adjectives from it.

spendulum → spendulous → spendulistic
The test of a good coinage

Guessable (a stranger can work out roughly what it means), sayable (it doesn't trip the tongue), and specific (it names one pattern, not a whole mood). Spendulum passes all three, which is why it grew into a whole family.

The Founding Word

The spendulum swing

The lexicon began with a single question: what do you call someone who swings from overspending to extreme penny-pinching? Four candidate words were tried, and spendulum won because the image of a pendulum explains itself.

Real Terms & Global Idioms

What already exists, checked against sources

The archive also gathered real terms and sayings from other languages. Invented words are clearly invented; real ones should be verifiably real. Here is the honest status of each.

Corrections to the archive

Tornak: I could not find this word in Turkish with this meaning. The close-sounding real word tırnak means fingernail or claw. Treat "tornak" as unverified until a Turkish speaker confirms it.

French phrase: pince-maille is a real word for a miser, but gros mangeur means "big eater", not "big spender". The phrase reads as a literal word-for-word translation rather than an actual French idiom.

Enantiodromia: the idea traces back to Heraclitus, and Jung made it a named principle in analytical psychology (his clearest definition is in Psychological Types, 1921). The archive's claim that Heraclitus himself "coined" the word, and the 1912 date, are not well supported.

An interesting discovery

The Hindi/Urdu proverb is recorded as the equivalent of "penny wise and pound foolish". That describes doing both at the same time, not swinging from one to the other. So the proverb is actually an older name for faux-gal (Entry 003), not for spendulum. Your coinage had a centuries-old relative you hadn't connected it to.

Active Recall

Five checks before you close the tab

Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. The aim is to rebuild the idea from memory, not just recognise it.

1 · Name the four techniques used to coin words in this lexicon.

Blend (spendulum), hyphenated compound (cart-amnesia), eponym (Plutus-flip), and affix family (spendulous, spendulistic).

2 · What is the difference between "spendulous" and "spendulistic"?

Spendulous describes the swing itself, a single abrupt move from overspending to parsimony. Spendulistic describes a recurring cycle: five-star dinners in week one, plain lentils in week four.

3 · Which existing proverb turned out to match faux-gal better than spendulum, and why?

Ashrafi lutayein aur koylon par mohar, meaning "penny wise and pound foolish". It describes doing both at once, which is the faux-gal pattern, rather than a swing from one extreme to the other.

4 · What are the three tests of a good coinage?

Guessable, sayable, specific. A word that fails "specific" ends up naming a mood instead of a pattern you can catch yourself doing.

5 · How is mandi-mirage related to payday-phoria?

Both describe a false sense of wealth when cash arrives. Payday-phoria is the salaried version, which vanishes once rent clears. Mandi-mirage is the seasonal farm version, which vanishes once input loans, dues and next season's seed are subtracted. Same illusion, different cash cycle.
